## Comments of Expert 1 — XU11124/Zhang

What I like: constraining nuclear shape and the neutron skin from a single UPC observable, rather than one at a time, is a good question and a timely one. The construction is sensible, and the Prediction Difference Analysis result, that deformation sits in the dark fringes and the skin in the bright-spot edges, is a clean way to expose what the network uses. It is also consistent with their Eqs. (2) and (3) and with the direct ratios in Fig. 6. I have no complaint about the machine learning.

My main concerns are:

**1.** The proposed deformation signal sits in a kinematic region that is swamped by detector resolution. Section IV B places it at pT < 0.005 GeV/c, i.e. 5 MeV/c. At midrapidity the J/psi is reconstructed from two daughters of about 1.5 GeV/c, so their momentum errors add in quadrature into the pair pT. Even 0.5% per-track resolution gives a pair-momentum scale of about 10 MeV/c before angular resolution enters, which is twice the full signal window. Because the response depends on daughter kinematics, it also reshapes the two-dimensional pattern rather than simply blurring it. The paper claims robustness against detector smearing, but I found no detector response anywhere in the methods or appendices. This needs a detector-level calculation with realistic present-day input; Kesler et al. (arXiv:2502.15596) treat exactly this resolution problem as the central issue for the analogous e+A measurement.

**A:**


**2.** There is no statistical projection, and the required yields look very far out of reach. The deformation effect in Fig. 8 is a 2% change in a v2 of about 4.7 x 10^-3, so the absolute change is of order 10^-4. On a back-of-the-envelope estimate with a perfect event plane and perfect efficiency, resolving that at 3 sigma needs of order 10^8 to 10^9 coherent J/psi inside the signal window. By the paper's own numbers, that window holds only 0.16% of the yield below 0.17 GeV/c, which puts the total requirement above 10^11 coherent J/psi. A serious projection has to fold in the coherent yield and branching fraction, acceptance, efficiency, momentum resolution, background and event-plane resolution, and then be compared against projected LHC Run 4 and Run 5 luminosities. This is needed to support the claim of "analysis-ready observables for future high-luminosity data."

**A:**


**3.** The network can only be as good as the model that generated its training data. Current model contains smooth deformed Woods-Saxon densities, three prescribed neutron-density classes, orientation averaging and a Glauber incoherent component. It omits event-by-event nucleon and subnucleon fluctuations, alternative density profiles spanning nuclear-structure theory uncertainty, finite event counts, detector response, low-pT acceptance and non-UPC backgrounds. Several of these modify the same low-pT structure the network could mistreat as deformation or skin, so they do not merely add uncertainty, they bias the inference in the same direction as the signal. Table I merely rescales the same incoherent template by a constant, which is a robustness check against normalization, not against a different generator. The reported R^2 values and the 99.3% classification accuracy therefore show only that the network can invert its own input. Relatedly, classifying three prescribed templates as skin-type, halo-type or none is not a measurement of Delta r_np.

**A:**


**4.** Every interference image is computed at one fixed impact parameter, b = 12 fm. The fringe scale enters Eq. (2) through b and varies as 1/b: the first interference zero moves from about 0.10 GeV/c at b = 12 fm to about 0.04 GeV/c at b = 30 fm, so across a realistic impact-parameter distribution the fringes sweep through most of the 0.17 GeV/c analysis window. Averaging over nuclear orientation does not address this. Closely related work including Zr+Zr, Phys. Rev. C 109, 024908 (2024), finds that the treatment of the minimum impact parameter can dominate the inferred geometry dependence. The analysis should be repeated with photon-flux- and selection-weighted impact-parameter distributions, preferably within defined neutron-tag classes.

**A:**


**5.** The v2 and v3 in Figs. 8 and 9 are not defined, and no event-plane direction is given. The simulated fringe pattern is aligned with the known b direction, which is not accessible event by event in a UPC. If the plotted harmonics are computed relative to the true b axis, the paper needs to supply an experimentally reconstructible estimator of that axis together with its resolution. Relative to a fixed laboratory axis these harmonics average to zero. As written, they are not experimental observables.

**A:**


**6.** On novelty, the new element is the inference layer rather than the underlying sensitivity. That different deformation multipoles affect different momentum-transfer regions was shown in Phys. Rev. Lett. 131, 062301 (2023), and Phys. Rev. C 109, 024908 (2024) studied nuclear geometry and interference in Zr+Zr UPCs. It would be very useful if the manuscript can compare against a direct template fit, a likelihood fit or Bayesian inference on the same input, so the unique gain from the multitask network can be quantified. The appeal's narrower claim of joint identifiability is fair, but it holds only inside one simplified generator.

## Comments of Expert 2 — XU11124/Zhang

### 1 Core Idea and Physics Context

The manuscript starts from a physically sound and attractive observation. In a symmetric heavy-ion ultra-peripheral collision (UPC) such as

$$
{}^{96}\mathrm{Zr}+{}^{96}\mathrm{Zr}\to{}^{96}\mathrm{Zr}+J/\psi+{}^{96}\mathrm{Zr}, \qquad (1)
$$

either nucleus can act as the photon emitter while the other acts as the photoproduction target. At or near midrapidity, the two amplitudes can be comparable, so one must add them coherently. Schematically,

$$
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{p}_T,\mathbf{b})\sim
\mathcal{M}_1e^{+i\mathbf{p}_T\cdot\mathbf{b}/2}
-\mathcal{M}_2e^{-i\mathbf{p}_T\cdot\mathbf{b}/2}, \qquad (2)
$$

and the cross section contains the familiar two-source interference term.
The manuscript writes the corresponding momentum-space probability schematically as

$$
\frac{d^2P}{d^2p_T}\propto
\left|\mathcal{F}\{A_1(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{b}/2)-A_2(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{b}/2)\}\right|^2. \qquad (3)
$$

For identical spherical nuclei at fixed impact parameter, the authors motivate an approximate separation into a nuclear form-factor/diffraction envelope and a double-source interference factor. The physical interpretation is that the radial density profile primarily modifies the diffraction envelope, whereas deformation induces event-by-event orientation-dependent phase and amplitude differences that preferentially alter the interference minima.

This is the physics behind the paper’s “bright spots versus dark fringes” language:

- Neutron-surface/radial-density information is claimed to reside primarily in the diffraction envelope and the edges of the bright region.
- Quadrupole and octupole deformation are claimed to reside primarily in the interference minima or “dark fringes.”

The machine-learning component is then an inverse mapper. The authors generate Glauber–vector-meson-dominance (Glauber–VMD) calculations using deformed Woods–Saxon distributions,

$$
R(\theta,\phi)=R_0[1+\beta_2Y_{20}(\theta,\phi)+\beta_3Y_{30}(\theta,\phi)], \qquad (4)
$$

and implement neutron structure either through a larger neutron radius (“skin”) or larger neutron diffuseness (“halo”). The resulting $p_x,p_y$ images are fed to a ResNet-34 with a common feature-extraction backbone and separate output heads for $\beta_2$, $\beta_3$, and neutron-skin class.

Within this synthetic model ensemble, the quoted performance is numerically impressive: approximately $R^2=0.96$ for $\beta_2$, $R^2=0.93$ for $\beta_3$, and about 99% skin-type classification accuracy at the authors’ nominal incoherent-background level. They also multiply the incoherent component by a factor of 100 and retain $R^2\simeq0.78$ for $\beta_2$, $R^2\simeq0.72$ for $\beta_3$, and roughly 85% classification accuracy.

The genuinely useful part comes next. Prediction-difference analysis indicates that the network uses different parts of momentum space for different tasks: the deformation heads concentrate on the interference minima, while neutron-skin classification concentrates around the diffraction peaks. Direct spectrum ratios reproduce the same qualitative separation. The authors, therefore, step back from the neural network and propose conventional observables inspired by it:

- a bright-region width/area or $\langle p_T\rangle$ for radial structure;
- yield in the very-low-$p_T$ interference region and Fourier harmonics for deformation;
- higher-$p_T$ harmonics around $0.14<p_T<0.16$ GeV/c for neutron-surface discrimination.

The manuscript specifically proposes a yield below $p_T=0.005$ GeV/c and high-$p_T$ observables in $0.14<p_T<0.16$ GeV/c.

In my view, this is the real scientific content of the work. The ResNet itself is not the essential result. The result is the observation, within one forward model, that two structural degrees of freedom project preferentially into different regions of the interference/diffraction pattern.

**A:**


### 1.1 Where this sits in heavy-ion and nuclear-structure physics

There are several levels of physics layered together here.

In conventional relativistic A+A collisions, nuclear shape influences the initial overlap geometry, which subsequently becomes encoded in quark-gluon-plasma collective flow. That strategy has become sophisticated enough to provide genuinely new nuclear-structure information; STAR’s recent nuclear-shape imaging work is an example of an experimentally demonstrated technique in which high-energy collisions constrain nuclear geometry [2].

UPCs offer something conceptually cleaner. For $b\gtrsim R_1+R_2$, hadronic overlap is strongly suppressed and the Lorentz-boosted Coulomb field can be treated as a flux of quasi-real photons. Coherent photoproduction,

$$
\gamma+A\to V+A, \qquad (5)
$$

then produces a low-$p_T$ vector meson whose momentum-transfer distribution is Fourier-related to a transverse spatial distribution.

For a light vector meson such as the $\rho^0$, this has long been exploited as nuclear diffraction and two-source interference. The Fermi-scale double-slit interpretation is established prior art, including theoretical work by Zha and collaborators and experimental STAR measurements of interference and polarization phenomena [3, 4].

For a $J/\psi$, however, the short $c\bar c$ scale makes the process primarily a probe of the nuclear gluon distribution. The physical chain is therefore represented as

$$
\rho_n(\mathbf r),\rho_p(\mathbf r)
\to\text{nucleon geometry}
\to\text{spatial gluon distribution + shadowing}
\to\gamma A\to J/\psi A\to p_T. \qquad (6)
$$

A $J/\psi$ $p_T$ distribution is thus not literally the Fourier transform of the neutron density.

Neutron skin can influence the $J/\psi$ form factor through the assumed spatial distribution of nucleons, but only through a model for how the nuclear gluon distribution follows that geometry. Current STAR UPC analyses explicitly describe coherent $J/\psi$ photoproduction as a probe of transverse gluon structure and fluctuations; the relevant RHIC kinematics are in the approximate range $x\sim0.005$–$0.04$ [1].

This makes the manuscript’s frequent language about the spectrum directly encoding the “neutron distribution” too strong. It encodes a model-dependent gluonic response to the assumed neutron/proton geometry.

The two-source interferometer itself is not new either. The manuscript’s narrower new point is more credible: if neutron-surface structure and deformation are varied simultaneously rather than one at a time, can they still be disentangled after orientation averaging and incoherent contamination? That is essentially how the appeal now formulates the claimed advance. I regard that as a legitimate contribution, but it is substantially narrower than discovering a Fermi-scale interferometer or establishing a new connection between coherent imaging and nuclear physics.

### 2 Limitations and Experimental Feasibility

#### 2.1

The visually striking $p_x,p_y$ patterns are calculated at midrapidity and a specified impact parameter, with the impact-parameter vector implicitly defining an axis. The authors average over nuclear Euler orientations, but the explicit images remain b-aligned theoretical momentum distributions.

A real UPC sample does not come with a laboratory-frame b axis. The impact parameter is random event to event and is not directly measured. Simply averaging laboratory $p_x,p_y$ over many events washes out an anisotropy tied to an unknown direction. STAR’s current $J/\psi$ interference work deals with this experimentally through a different observable involving the $J/\psi$ decay kinematics and polarization/spin information rather than by directly reconstructing a b-aligned two-dimensional momentum image [1].

Correspondingly, the manuscript introduces $v_2$ and $v_3$ but does not provide an operational experimental definition of the reference plane. There is no explicit event plane, no reconstructed b direction, no decay-plane estimator, and no treatment of the associated resolution. In an exclusive UPC, there is essentially no midrapidity underlying event from which to construct the usual heavy-ion event plane.

**A:**


#### 2.2

The paper explicitly acknowledges that neutron tagging changes the effective impact-parameter distribution, but the displayed calculations are centered on a representative configuration, $y=0$ and $b=12$ fm, while the methods discussion emphasizes averaging over nuclear Euler orientations rather than a fully realistic experimentally selected UPC impact-parameter ensemble.

If the training truly uses fixed $b=12$ fm, that is a major deficiency. If the authors integrate over b elsewhere in the implementation, it needs to be stated and documented explicitly.

The interference phase depends directly on

$$
\mathbf{p}_T\cdot\mathbf b, \qquad (7)
$$

so changing the b distribution changes the depth and location of precisely the interference minima proposed as deformation observables.

Similarly, exactly at $y=0$ the two photon-emitter amplitudes are maximally symmetric. Across STAR’s finite rapidity acceptance, the two photon energies and the two source amplitudes become unequal, reducing or reshaping the interference. A detector-level analysis needs something closer to

$$
P(b,y,\text{neutron class},p_T,\phi) \qquad (8)
$$

rather than one representative $(b,y)$ point. This is particularly relevant to the isobar data because STAR subdivides UPC events into forward-neutron-emission classes, and those classes carry different impact-parameter weighting [1].

**A:**


#### 2.3

The manuscript at one point states that the method can simultaneously extract $\beta_2$ and the neutron-skin thickness. However, the network does not regress a continuous quantity such as

$$
\Delta r_{np}=r_n-r_p. \qquad (9)
$$

Instead, it classifies three model categories: approximately “halo,” “skin,” and “none.” The nuclear model itself defines those categories by changing either neutron radius or neutron diffuseness. These are substantially different scientific statements.

A 99% ability to distinguish three deliberately constructed model families means:

“Given spectra generated from these three prescribed Woods–Saxon families, the classifier can identify which family generated the spectrum.”

It does not mean:

“A real STAR spectrum can determine $\Delta r_{np}$ to useful precision after marginalizing over gluon shadowing, proton radius, diffuseness, nucleon correlations, subnucleonic fluctuations, deformation, impact parameter, detector response, and backgrounds.”

For PRC, this can be addressed by changing the claims and, preferably, adding continuous inference. For PRX it is a much more serious mismatch between the headline physics and what has actually been demonstrated.

**A:**


#### 2.4

The simulation uses one Glauber–VMD mapping from nuclear density to photoproduction amplitude. The neural network is then trained and tested on the same family of models. An $R^2$ near 0.96 under those conditions demonstrates interpolation within the simulator; it does not establish identifiability in Nature.

A serious extraction would have to vary, at minimum:

- the gluon-shadowing prescription;
- nucleon-level gluon profiles;
- possible subnucleonic fluctuations;
- photon transverse-momentum distribution and nuclear charge form factor;
- longitudinal coherence corrections;
- proton and neutron density parametrizations beyond the chosen Woods–Saxon family;
- nucleon correlations;
- alternative photoproduction descriptions, e.g. representative dipole/CGC approaches;
- normalization and t-slope uncertainties.

This is particularly important because the modern experimental interpretation of coherent $J/\psi$ production is primarily in terms of gluon structure. A genuinely PRX-level claim of “neutron-skin tomography” would, in my opinion, require evidence that the putative skin-sensitive feature remains identifiable across a broad theory ensemble rather than only within one density-to-$J/\psi$ mapping.

**A:**


#### 2.5

The authors place much of the deformation discrimination in the deepest interference minimum. The proposed low-$p_T$ yield uses

$$
Y(p_T<0.005\ \mathrm{GeV}/c) \qquad (10)
$$

relative to a much broader coherent region.

Five MeV/c is an extremely aggressive experimental scale for a $J/\psi\to e^+e^-$ measurement at STAR. Each electron carries transverse momentum at roughly the GeV scale; the tiny pair momentum is obtained through cancellation of two much larger measured track momenta. Material effects and bremsstrahlung, tracking curvature resolution, alignment, and acceptance therefore enter directly.

I would not say that a $p_T<5$ MeV/c integral is mathematically impossible. However, before calling it an experimental observable, the authors need to demonstrate migration across that boundary using a realistic STAR response matrix and realistic finite statistics. The present paper does not do that.

**A:**


#### 2.6

The proposed neutron-surface observables use

$$
0.14<p_T<0.16\ \mathrm{GeV}/c. \qquad (11)
$$

This is not primarily a detector-resolution problem, but it lies toward the edge/tail of the coherent nuclear distribution, where several effects become more important:

- coherent statistics decrease;
- incoherent $J/\psi$ production becomes relatively more important;
- the result becomes increasingly sensitive to coherent/incoherent template modeling;
- t-dependent shadowing and gluon-structure uncertainties matter more.

STAR’s existing isobar analyses obtain the coherent yield through simultaneous fits using coherent, incoherent, and QED components passed through detector simulation [5]. Treating incoherent production simply as a diffuse additive “background” is useful as a first theoretical stress test, but it is not sufficient for a precision structure extraction in exactly the $p_T$ region where the coherent/incoherent mixture is changing rapidly.

**A:**
